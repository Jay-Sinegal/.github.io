How to Pitch and Promote Music Responsibly

Trust is part of the product. Authority, transparent compensation, correct licensing, and clean records protect both the opportunity and the people who created the work.

By Jaylen Sinegal · September 4, 2026

A good connection can help a song travel. It does not create the right to represent, distribute, post, perform, synchronize, or monetize that song. Anyone building opportunities around music should make the chain of permission as professional as the pitch itself.

Start with written authority

Before pitching or promoting a specific work, identify who owns or controls the interests involved and obtain written authorization from the artist, songwriter, publisher, label, or other appropriate rights holder. The document should name the work, define exactly what you may do, identify the people or companies you may approach, state the territory and term, and explain whether the authority is exclusive or nonexclusive.

Do not describe yourself as an owner, publisher, manager, agent, or exclusive representative unless a valid agreement actually gives you that role. Permission to make introductions is not automatically permission to license the work or collect money for its use.

Separate the song from the recording

A recorded song can contain two separately protected works: the underlying musical composition and the sound recording. Those interests may have different owners and may require different approvals. The U.S. Copyright Office explains this distinction in its Copyright for Musicians guidance.

That means one signature may not clear every intended use. Confirm ownership and authority for both sides before representing that a track is cleared.

Put every fee and commission in the agreement

State how you will be paid before accepting music or money. Define whether compensation is a flat fee, retainer, reimbursement, percentage commission, or a combination; what event earns it; when it becomes payable; how long it applies; and whether expenses require advance approval. Also say who receives incoming funds and how statements and records will be provided.

A commission should compensate documented services. It should not quietly redirect copyright ownership or royalties that belong to creators and rights holders.

Match the licenses to the actual use

“Promotion” is not a universal license. A website stream, social video, advertisement, podcast, live event, download, physical release, interactive stream, film placement, or brand campaign can implicate different rights. Define the exact media, platform, edit, audience, territory, duration, paid-media plan, and monetization before anyone promises clearance.

The Music Modernization Act created a blanket mechanical licensing system for certain digital uses by eligible digital music providers; it does not clear every possible promotional or commercial use. The U.S. Copyright Office’s Music Modernization Act overview explains that scope. Have qualified counsel or a licensing professional identify and secure every permission the proposed use requires.

Keep royalties flowing to the correct owners

Preserve accurate song titles, writer and publisher shares, ownership notices, performer and producer credits, and identifiers supplied by the rights holders. Do not change splits, registrations, payee instructions, metadata, or collection arrangements without documented authority. Provide timely deal records, usage reports, payment statements, and copies of licenses to the people entitled to them.

For eligible U.S. digital mechanical royalties under the statutory blanket license, songwriters and publishers should keep their data current with The Mechanical Licensing Collective. The Copyright Office describes The MLC’s role in collecting, matching, and distributing those royalties in its Music Modernization Act FAQ. Other uses and royalty streams may be administered elsewhere.

Use a written intake gate

Before accepting a submission, ask for the legal names and contact details of the submitting party, the song and recording titles, ownership and split information, publisher and label details, relevant identifiers, existing agreements or restrictions, requested opportunity, and confirmation that the submitter has authority to provide the material. Do not invite confidential material through a general contact form.

Let counsel review the deal before value changes hands

An entertainment attorney should review the authorization and compensation terms before you accept music, begin pitching, collect a fee, receive licensing revenue, or promise a use. That review can surface conflicts involving exclusivity, agency or procurement rules, ownership, term, accounting, approvals, indemnity, termination, and governing law.

Jaylen’s rights-first standard: obtain written authority for the specific work; disclose and document every fee or commission; secure every license required for the intended use; preserve accurate credits, ownership data, and royalty pathways; and require entertainment-counsel review before accepting music or money.
